Four Views of One Attack Surface

Sightline, Blind Spots, Microsoft Defender EASM and Hadrian all describe an organisation's external exposure — but two of them look inward from the internet and two look outward from the tenant. That difference decides how fast an inventory arrives, what each can find, what it costs, and whether they compete at all.

Where each one stands

Every difference further down follows from this one. It is not a feature gap — it is a difference in vantage point, and each vantage answers a question the other cannot.

Internet → you

Outside-in discovery

Starts from seeds you supply — a domain, an IP block, an ASN — and crawls outward through DNS, certificates and observed connections to infer what else you own. It finds assets nobody told it about, which is the whole point.

The limit is symmetrical: it sees only what the internet answers. It cannot know whether anything is defending what it found, because that fact lives inside your tenant.

Because it is inference, the inventory converges rather than arrives — first results can be quick, but a discovery run is measured in hours or days as the recursion widens.

Defender EASM · Hadrian
You → internet

Inside-out correlation

Starts from the inventory your tenant already holds — Defender devices, Azure public IPs, Entra named locations — so the authoritative list lands in seconds rather than being inferred over days. It then asks which of these is unwatched.

The limit is also symmetrical: it can only reason about assets your tenant knows about. An acquired subsidiary's forgotten domain is invisible to it.

Because it is a lookup rather than inference, the inventory arrives complete — bounded by an API call, not by how far a crawl has got.

Sightline · Blind Spots
These are not substitutes. Sightline imports Defender EASM's inventory precisely because outside-in discovery produces addresses the tenant sources cannot. The honest framing is two questions: what do I own that I don't know about? and what of what I own is exposed and unwatched? No single tool here answers both well.

Can anyone promise complete coverage?

The question a board asks, and the one where an overclaim does the most damage. The short answer is that no tool here can promise 100% — but the three fail differently, and only one of them can tell you the size of what it missed. Blind Spots is left out of this comparison deliberately: it performs no discovery at all, so its coverage is exactly the two files you exported, no more and no less.

Outside-in

The gap cannot be measured

Ownership is inferred from DNS records, certificates, ASN and WHOIS relationships. An asset with no DNS name, no certificate and an IP block that traces to nobody is simply not reachable by that method — and nothing in the product knows it exists to report it missing. There is no denominator, so there is no percentage to quote.

Defender EASM says as much in its own data model: discovered assets are labelled Approved Inventory, Candidate or Requires Investigation, and the last two exist because the system cannot decide ownership on its own.1 That is a confidence scale, not a guarantee.

Inside-out

The gap has a name

Sightline does not infer ownership — it reads the registries that already hold the answer. Within a scope you can state out loud, the list is complete because Azure, Defender and Arc are authoritative about their own contents.

That scope is: every Azure subscription the signed-in identity can read, every Defender-onboarded device, every Arc-connected server, Entra named locations and Cloud Apps subnets. A subscription nobody granted you read on is outside it — which is a permissions question with a known answer, not an unknown unknown.

The case that separates them

An abstract argument about vantage points is easy to nod along to and hard to act on. Here is the concrete instance, and it is the most common shadow-IT shape in a Microsoft estate.

A developer spins up a VM in a subscription you can read. Azure allocates it a public IP. There is no DNS record pointing at it, no certificate naming your organisation, and one port is listening. Nothing on the internet links that address to you.

  • Sightlineinside-out
    Detected
    The public IP is an Azure resource the moment it is allocated, so it lands in inventory from the Resource Graph query — before anything is scanned, and with no DNS name required. Sightline then scans it, finds the open port, and correlates: no Defender sensor behind it, so it reports as an exposed address in the coverage gap.
  • Blind Spotsinside-out
    Address only
    Your next Azure Public IP addresses export contains it, so it appears as an address with no sensor and shows up under new addresses in the trend against your last baseline. It will not tell you a port is open — it never scans anything.
  • Defender EASMoutside-in
    Missed
    Attribution runs along observable edges: domain → IP from DNS records, and certificate → IP from TLS.1 With neither present there is no edge from any of your seeds to this address. Nor does the ASN help: the block belongs to Microsoft, not to you, so walking it would attribute half of Azure.
  • Hadrianoutside-in
    Missed
    Same structural reason. Discovery begins from the domains you authorise, and this address is connected to none of them.
Two honest qualifications. The outside-in tools are not permanently blind — they catch it the moment it becomes linkable: someone adds a DNS record, it starts presenting a certificate with your domain, or the Azure DNS label carries your company name. Much shadow IT eventually crosses that line, so the gap is one of timing, not of forever. And Sightline only sees this address because the subscription is one the signed-in identity can read: a developer using a subscription outside your tenant is invisible to every tool on this page.

An "asset" is not the same unit in both products. Defender EASM counts approved host:IP combinations, domains and IP addresses, and de-duplicates — a subdomain resolving to the same IP as its parent is not counted twice, and an IP is only billable if a port or certificate was observed in the last 30 days.3 A Hadrian asset count is defined by Hadrian. Two quotes for the same estate can therefore differ before any price is applied, so compare counted assets, not just rates.

Which to reach for

Start hereBlind Spots

Two exports and five minutes tell you whether you have a coverage problem worth funding. Free, passive, and it answers the question that justifies everything else.

Own the estateSightline

When the coverage gap is real and needs continuous scanning, correlation, trending and reporting — and when the inventory that matters is already in your tenant.

Find the unknownDefender EASM

When the worry is assets nobody remembers owning. Cheapest per asset by a wide margin, and it imports directly into Sightline rather than competing with it.

Prove exploitabilityHadrian

When a list of findings is not enough and someone needs proof a path is exploitable. The premium buys validation and pentest, not inventory.

Where the Blue16 tools are the weaker choice. Sightline is self-hosted software that runs an elevated scanner — that is real operational and security cost a managed service does not impose on you, and it is a restricted-preview product from a small vendor rather than a supported service from Microsoft or a funded platform vendor, which matters for continuity, support and roadmap. Blind Spots refreshes only when a person re-exports. And neither Blue16 tool proves exploitability the way Hadrian's validation does: they report what is reachable and unwatched, which is a different and lesser claim than "here is how it is exploited".
